## Cold open

> SCENE (line): A paper lantern glowing in the dark.
> lantern | Every November, Germany lights up.

Every November, as it gets dark, thousands of children all over Germany walk through the streets with paper lanterns, singing. Someone in a red cloak rides ahead of them on a horse.

> SCENE (photo): A goose dinner, then the Düsseldorf carnival crowd.
> goose | | color
> carnival | | color @ Karneval

Afterwards, half the country eats roast goose. And on the same day, in Cologne and Düsseldorf, Karneval begins. It all looks unrelated. It's one story. And it starts with a cloak, cut in two.

> SCENE (title): Title card.
> HIDDEN DEUTSCHLAND · MARTINSTAG

This is [[Martinstag]]. Saint Martin's Day, the eleventh of November.

## The soldier and the beggar

> SCENE (photo): El Greco's Saint Martin and the Beggar.
> elgreco | El Greco, Saint Martin and the Beggar, 1597–1599 | color from 0.5 0.35 1.0 to 0.5 0.4 1.12

Martin was a Roman soldier, in the fourth century. The story goes that on a freezing night, he rode past a beggar at the gate of a town. He had nothing to give, except his cloak.

So he took his sword, cut the cloak in two, and gave the beggar half.

> SCENE (place): Martin's tomb in Tours.
> tours | TOURS, FRANCE | Martin's tomb, under the Basilica of Saint Martin

That night, the legend says, Martin dreamed of Jesus, wearing the half cloak. He left the army, became a monk, and later the bishop of Tours, in France. He was buried on the eleventh of November, in the year 397. That's why his day is the eleventh.

## The lanterns

> SCENE (photo): A Martin procession at night, then the rider.
> procession | A Martinsumzug in Lower Saxony | color
> martin-horse | Saint Martin and his horse, Düsseldorf | color @ dressed as Martin

Today, the [[Laternenumzug]], the lantern procession, is the part most people see. Children make their own lanterns, often at kindergarten or school, and walk together after dark, singing songs like [[Laterne, Laterne]]. In many towns, someone dressed as Martin rides ahead on a horse.

> SCENE (line): The Martinsfeuer at the end of a procession.
> fire | Martin's kindness brought light into the dark.

At the end, there's often a [[Martinsfeuer]], a big fire, and the cloak scene is acted out. The light is the whole point. Martin's kindness, the idea goes, brought light into the dark. And in November in Germany, it's dark by five.

## The goose

> SCENE (photo): A flock of geese.
> geese | | color

Why goose? There's a legend for that too. When the people of Tours wanted Martin as their bishop, he didn't want the job. So he hid in a barn full of geese. The geese honked so loudly that he was found, and made bishop anyway. The goose on your plate, the story says, is their punishment.

> SCENE (line): Martini was a day for paying rent and taxes.
> goose | Martini: the old day for paying rents and taxes.

The real reason is more practical. [[Martini]], the eleventh of November, was an old date for paying rents, taxes and wages, after the harvest. Many people paid in kind. And a goose, fattened over the summer, was a common way to pay.

## Why Karneval starts on 11.11

> SCENE (list): From 11 November to Christmas: a time of fasting.
> 11 November | the last big feast
> Until Christmas | weeks of fasting @ fasting
> 11.11 at 11:11 | Karneval begins @ eleven minutes past eleven

Here's the part almost nobody knows. In the Middle Ages, the weeks from Saint Martin's Day to Christmas were a time of fasting, a bit like Lent before Easter. It was even called Saint Martin's Lent.

So the eleventh of November was the last big feast before weeks of going without. Eat the goose. Finish the meat. Celebrate.

> SCENE (place): Düsseldorf on 11 November.
> carnival | COLOGNE · DÜSSELDORF · MAINZ | 11 November, 11:11

A last party before the fast. That's exactly what Karneval is. So when Cologne, Düsseldorf and Mainz open the carnival season on the eleventh of the eleventh, at eleven minutes past eleven, they're building on that old feast day. And the eleven? It's the fool's number. One more than the Ten Commandments, one less than the twelve apostles.

## Sweet bread men

> SCENE (photo): A Weckmann.
> weckmann | | color

Around the same time, bakeries sell little men made of sweet bread. In the Rhineland, they're called a [[Weckmann]]. In Westphalia, a [[Stutenkerl]]. Same little man, a different name almost everywhere you go.

## How to join

> SCENE (line): Anyone can walk along.
> procession | Search your town for "Martinsumzug" or "Laternenumzug".

You don't need to be religious, German, or a child to take part. Kindergartens, schools and churches organise most processions, and many are open to everyone. Look on your town's website for [[Martinsumzug]] or [[Laternenumzug]]. Bring a lantern. Learn the first line of [[Laterne, Laterne]]. That's all.

## The takeaway

> SCENE (line): One cloak, cut in two. Sixteen hundred years of lanterns.
> lantern | One cloak, cut in two. Sixteen hundred years of lanterns.

One cold night, a cloak cut in two. And more than sixteen hundred years later, a whole country goes out into the dark with lanterns.
