## What a picture can prove

> SCENE (figure): The Hortus Deliciarum, the banquet of Esther.
> hortus | Hortus Deliciarum, late 12th century | 0.60 0.545 @ pretzel-shaped bread
> The Garden of Delights: an encyclopedia made for the nuns of Hohenburg, Alsace @ illustrated encyclopedia
> A banquet scene with Queen Esther @ banquet scene
> Thought to be the earliest known picture of a pretzel @ earliest known picture

[calm] Here is the oldest evidence we found. In the late twelfth century, an abbess called Herrad of Landsberg compiled an illustrated encyclopedia for the young nuns of her convent at [[Hohenburg]], in Alsace. It's called the [[Hortus Deliciarum]], the Garden of Delights. In a banquet scene with the Bible's Queen Esther, there's a pretzel-shaped bread. It's thought to be the earliest known picture of a pretzel. Sources date the book between about 1167 and 1185, and one says as late as 1195.

> SCENE (line): The original burned in 1870.
> The original burned in 1870. Only copies survive.

[warmly] But the original is gone. In 1870, during the siege of Strasbourg, the library holding it was bombarded, and the manuscript burned. What survives are copies, drawn in 1818 and printed between 1879 and 1899. We couldn't find out which copy our pretzel picture comes from.

> SCENE (line): A picture shows a shape. It can't show lye.
> A picture shows a shape. It can't show lye.

[calm] And a picture has limits. It shows a shape. It can't tell us whether anyone dipped it in lye.

> SCENE (receipt): Seals.
> Stiftung Warentest, 22 February 2017 | Laugenbrezeln im Test
> ==Bereits um 1300== taucht die Brezel auf den Zunftsiegeln der Bäcker auf. | Also stated by Austria's agriculture ministry: early 14th century @ around 1300

By around 1300, the pretzel appears on the seals of bakers' guilds. That's the word of [[Stiftung Warentest]] and Austria's ministry; neither shows its own evidence. By then, the pretzel was a symbol of the whole trade. The name most likely comes from a Latin word for "arm". Most likely.

## The lab

> SCENE (photo): The lye dip.
> lye-dip |  | color

[calm] Now the lab. The pretzel is dipped briefly in sodium hydroxide, lye, before baking.

> SCENE (receipt): How much lye.
> Austrian Food Codex and a German working paper (GDCh, 2015) | How strong is the lye?
> Austria: ==2 to 6 %== sodium hydroxide, brief dip | Austrian Food Codex B18, via the agriculture ministry @ two to six percent
> Germany: about ==4 %== is normal good practice | the legal maximum until 1998 @ about four percent
> Sodium hydroxide (E 524): =="quantum satis"== | no fixed upper limit: use what you need @ E five twenty-four

[calm] Austria's food code says two to six percent. A working paper from German food chemists says about four percent is normal good practice in Germany, which was also the legal maximum until 1998. Today the lye is a permitted additive, E five twenty-four, with no fixed upper limit: use what you need, no more. The lye stays on the surface.

> SCENE (receipt): The smell.
> Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, 2019 | Key aroma compounds in the crust of soft pretzels
> Sebastian Schoenauer and Peter Schieberle, Technical University of Munich | Chair of Food Chemistry
> The ==crust==, not the crumb, carries the smell | trained testers @ The smell is in the crust
> 28 smell compounds. Two stand out: ==caramel-like== and ==roasty, popcorn-like== | @ Out of twenty-eight
> The top ==six== recreate the smell in a lab | @ top six

[warmly] And the smell? In 2019, Sebastian [[Schoenauer]] and Peter [[Schieberle]], at the Technical University of Munich, published a study in the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry. Trained testers smelled the crust and the crumb separately. The smell is in the crust. Out of twenty-eight smell compounds, two stood out: one caramel-like, one roasty, like popcorn. Mix the top six, and you can recreate the pretzel smell in a lab.

> SCENE (line): We read the abstract, not the full paper.
> We read the abstract and the press release. Not the full paper.

[calm] Their press release says the lye is, in part, responsible for the taste, texture and smell. How exactly? We could only read the abstract and that press release, not the full paper. So we won't pretend to know.

## The hidden problem

> SCENE (photo): Lye and the tray.
> tray-lye |  | color

[calm] Here's a part I didn't expect. Lye eats aluminium, and the metal can end up on the pretzel.

> SCENE (receipt): The BfR statement.
> BfR, German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment | Statement of 25 November 2002: Increased aluminium in lye pastry
> Pastry baked fresh on ==aluminium trays== | North Rhine-Westphalia, sampled in 2000 @ North Rhine-Westphalia
> The lye ==gets enriched with aluminium== when trays are dipped in it, and then reused | Oldenburg state laboratory leaflet, 1995, cited by the BfR @ from the year 2000

[calm] In 2002, Germany's risk-assessment institute, the BfR, reviewed measurements from North Rhine-Westphalia, from the year 2000.

> SCENE (alubars): The bars.

In one set of forty-six samples, aluminium ranged from 1.4 to 82.8 milligrams per kilo, averaging 8.9. In another, fifty samples: 1.1 to 19.1, averaging 4.9. The bakers' association puts an average pretzel at forty grams. At the averages, that's two to four tenths of a milligram. The highest value, 3.3.

> SCENE (receipt): Then and now.
> BfR, 2002, and the GDCh working paper, 2015 | Then and now
> 2002: not a concern, against a ==1989 limit== | 7 mg per kg of body weight per week @ against the limit
> Today: EFSA limit ==1 mg== per kg per week | 2015 paper: a worst-case pretzel can reach or slightly pass a 20 kg child's day-share @ European limit
> Still judged harmless by current knowledge. Extra intake: unwanted. | Unprotected aluminium trays: ==no longer state of the art== @ no longer

[calm] In 2002 the BfR said that wasn't a concern, measured against the limit of the time. That limit has since come down. Using today's European limit, a 2015 paper by German food chemists calculates that a single worst-case pretzel could reach, or slightly pass, a child's daily share of the weekly limit. The same paper still calls the levels harmless by current knowledge, but says extra intake is unwanted, and that unprotected aluminium trays are no longer state of the art. 

> SCENE (photo): Steel trays or baking paper.
> tray-swap |  | color

Steel trays or baking paper do the job.

> SCENE (receipt): And now?
> Stiftung Warentest, 22 February 2017 | 17 pretzels tested
> Bavarian inspectors found high aluminium =="regelmäßig"== until recently | @ regularly found high aluminium
> ...Inzwischen ==nutzen viele Hersteller Edelstahlbleche== | the tested pretzels: low levels @ stainless steel

[warmly] And now? In 2017, Stiftung Warentest tested seventeen pretzels. It reports that until recently, Bavarian inspectors regularly found high aluminium in lye pretzels, and that many bakers now use stainless steel. The tested pretzels had low levels. We couldn't see the exact numbers; they're behind a paywall. So: an old, documented problem that seems to be under control. Not a reason to fear the pretzel you buy today, and we have no recent measurements either.

## On your plate

> SCENE (saltgauge): The salt gauge.

[calm] One more number. In that 2017 test, a frozen pretzel already had about one and a half grams of salt, without the coarse salt on top. That's a quarter of the maximum daily amount the German Nutrition Society recommends. Add the coarse salt, and it can be more than half. That's not a verdict, just a number worth knowing.
