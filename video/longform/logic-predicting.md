## The Berlin Wall, 1969

> SCENE (photo): The Berlin Wall in 1969; Berlin's TV tower.
> wall1969 | The Berlin Wall, 1969 | color
> berlin-tower2 |  | color @ how much longer

[warmly] In 1969, a young American physicist called Richard Gott stood in front of the Berlin Wall. It had been standing for eight years. And he asked himself a strange question: how much longer will it stand?

> SCENE (photo): Berlin's TV tower in fog.
> berlin-tower3 | Probably somewhere in the middle. | color key @ Most likely

[calm] He had no inside knowledge. So he reasoned like this. There's nothing special about the moment I'm here. So I'm probably not seeing the wall at the very beginning of its life, or at the very end. Most likely, I'm somewhere in the middle.

> SCENE (wall): The wall on a timeline: built 1961, Gott's visit 1969, his even-odds range.

[calm] From that, he worked out a range. There was an even chance, he said, that the wall would stand between two and two thirds more years, and twenty-four more years.

> SCENE (photo): Crowds at the Wall, November 1989.
> fall1989 | Twenty years later: right inside his range. | color key

[warmly] Twenty years later, in 1989, the wall came down. Right inside his range.

## The rule

> SCENE (photo): The Earth; a watch.
> earth |  | color
> watch2 | Expect it to last about as long again. | color key @ So expect

[calm] Gott called it the Copernican method. The idea is simple. If you know nothing else, assume you've arrived somewhere in the middle. So expect a thing to last about as long again as it has lasted so far.

> SCENE (photo): A band playing; an old shop window.
> guitar |  | color
> shop |  | color @ A shop

[calm] A band that has been together for two years? Expect about two more. A shop that has been open for forty years? Probably another forty.

> SCENE (photo): City lights; the Earth.
> city-night |  | color @ Gott published
> milky-way |  | color @ future of humanity

[calm] It sounds too simple. But when you really know nothing, it's the best guess you can make. Gott published it in the journal Nature in 1993, and used it on everything from Broadway shows to the future of humanity.

## Three kinds of waiting

> SCENE (line): Three kinds of waiting.
> Three kinds of waiting.

[calm] Of course, most of the time you do know something. And what you know changes the rule. There are three kinds of waiting.

> SCENE (photo): A football pitch; a pizza in the oven.
> football | Around an average: predict the average. | color key
> pizza |  | color @ pizza

One. **Things that cluster around an average.** How long a football match lasts. How long a pizza takes to bake. Here, predict the average. And if you're already past it, expect just a little more.

> SCENE (photo): A train waiting on a rainy platform.
> bw-platform | A long tail: the longer it lasts, the longer it lasts. | color key

Two. **Things with a long tail.** Most are short, but a few go on and on. How long a delay lasts. How long a meeting runs over. Here, the longer it has already lasted, the longer you should expect it to last.

> SCENE (photo): A shooting star.
> meteor | At random: the wait ahead stays the same. | color key
> milky-way |  | color @ shooting star

Three. **Things that happen at random.** When nothing remembers how long you've waited, the wait ahead stays the same, no matter how long you've been standing there. Waiting for a shooting star works like that.

> SCENE (waits): The three kinds of waiting, side by side.

[calm] So before you predict, ask one question: which kind of waiting is this?

## Your train

> SCENE (photo): A regional train; a dark platform.
> platform2 | 2025: only about 60% of long-distance trains on time. | color key
> dark-platform |  | color @ long tail

[calm] Back to the platform. In 2025, only about sixty percent of long-distance trains in Germany arrived on time. Delays happen. And they have a long tail: most are a few minutes, a few last for hours.

> SCENE (photo): A dark platform at night.
> dark-platform2 | 40 minutes late? Expect more. | color key
> watch |  | color @ the longer it has lasted

[calm] So a train that's five minutes late will probably be a few minutes late. But a train that's already forty minutes late? That's a different kind of delay. Something bigger has gone wrong, and the longer it has lasted, the more of it is still to come.

> SCENE (photo): A bus at night.
> bus | Late in a long delay? Look for the other way home. | color key @ That's when

[calm] That's why the board's "ten more minutes" deserves more trust at the start of a delay than an hour into it. And that's when it pays to look for the other train, the bus, or the hotel.

## The catch

> SCENE (photo): Scrolling the news.
> phone-news |  | color

[calm] There's one catch. Good predictions need good examples. Your sense of what's normal comes from what you've seen and heard.

> SCENE (photo): City traffic at night.
> traffic | Keep your examples honest. | color key

[warmly] And what we hear most about are the extremes: the eight-hour delay, the crash, the overnight success. If the stories you hear are unusual, your sense of normal will be off. So keep your examples honest.

## The takeaway

> SCENE (photo): A train pulling in.
> train-arrive | Assume the middle. Ask which kind of waiting. Don't trust a long delay. | color key

[calm] When you know almost nothing, assume you're somewhere in the middle. When you know a little, ask which kind of waiting it is. And when a delay has already lasted a long time, don't expect it to end soon.
