## The price of order

> SCENE (photo): Papers being filed; a pile of papers.
> files | You pay up front. You get paid back later. | color key
> papers |  | color @ you get paid back

[calm] Every bit of order has a price. You pay it up front, when you sort. And you get paid back later, when you search and find things faster.

> SCENE (line): The real question.
> Will I search enough to earn back the sorting?

[calm] So the real question is never "is order good?" It's "will I search enough to earn back the time I spent sorting?"

> SCENE (photo): Network cables.
> cables | Sorting gets expensive fast. | color key @ sorting gets expensive

Computer scientists ask this all the time. Sorting is one of the most studied problems in the field. And one of the first things they learned is that sorting gets expensive fast.

## Why sorting is expensive

> SCENE (sortcost): Ten books against a hundred, comparisons counting up.

[calm] Sort ten books by hand, and it's quick. Sort a hundred, and it takes much more than ten times as long. Every new item has to find its place among all the others. The bigger the pile, the more each new item costs.

> SCENE (photo): Hands searching through a box.
> box | Searching: you only pay when you need it. | color key

[calm] Searching is the other side. If you never sort, every search means looking through the pile. That's slow too, but you only pay when you actually need something.

> SCENE (list): The balance.
> bg | library
> Search often | Sort it @ Sort what you search often
> Search rarely | Leave it in a pile @ Leave in a pile

[calm] So the rule is about balance. Sort what you search often. Leave in a pile what you rarely look for.

## The rule

> SCENE (photo): A library.
> library2 | Thousands search it every day: sort it. | color key

[calm] Here's the rule. Order is worth it only for things you'll search many times. A library sorts its books, because thousands of people search them every day. A dictionary is in alphabetical order, because people look up words all the time. That's money well spent.

> SCENE (photo): A box of old receipts.
> receipts | Opened once a year? Don't sort it. | color key

But your own box of old receipts? You might open it once a year, if ever. Sorting it is effort you will probably never get back.

## The email study

> SCENE (place): IBM Research, California.
> office | SAN JOSE, CALIFORNIA | IBM Research, 2011

[warmly] In 2011, a team at IBM Research, led by Steve Whittaker, decided to measure this with email.

> 85,000 | times someone went back to find an email @ eighty-five thousand

[warmly] They followed three hundred and forty-five people at work, and logged more than eighty-five thousand moments when someone went back to find an old message.

> SCENE (photo): An office; a keyboard.
> office2 | Folders, or one big inbox? | color key

[calm] Some people kept dozens of folders. Others left everything in one big inbox and used search.

> SCENE (photo): Hands typing a search.

[calm] The results were clear. The people who filed carefully did use their folders to look for things. But the filing didn't help them find their messages more often. Search found what people wanted at least as well, and opening a folder was, if anything, a little slower than typing a search.

> SCENE (photo): A clock.
> clock | And that's before the time spent filing. | color key

And that's before counting the time spent filing in the first place. Every day, every email, dragged into its folder.

> SCENE (line): The conclusion.
> For email, search does the work.

[calm] The researchers' conclusion was polite, but plain. For finding emails, careful filing is mostly wasted effort. Search does the work.

## Where it breaks

> SCENE (list): When order still pays.
> bg | files2
> Others search it too | One person sorts, everyone saves @ Other people search it
> No search box | Paper: passport, tax, warranties @ There is no search box
> No word to search for | Keep a simple order by date @ You don't know what to search for

[calm] So should you throw your folders away? Not always. Order still pays in three cases. One. Other people search it too. A shared drive, a family's paperwork, a team's files. One person sorts, and everyone saves time.

Two. There is no search box. Paper doesn't have one. Your tax documents, your passport, the warranty for the washing machine. For things you'll need in a hurry, a simple, labelled folder is worth it.

Three. You don't know what to search for. Search works when you remember a word. When you only remember roughly when something happened, a simple order by date helps.

## Three everyday uses

> SCENE (photo): A laptop inbox.
> laptop3 | Email: stop filing. Search. | color key

Email. Stop filing. Keep one archive and search it. If you want, keep one or two folders for things that really matter, like contracts.

> SCENE (photo): A kitchen drawer.
> kitchen | Daily spoons: a fixed place. The garlic press: the jumble. | color key

[calm] The kitchen drawer. The spoons you use every day deserve a fixed place. The garlic press you use twice a year can live in the jumble.

> SCENE (photo): Scrolling photos on a phone.
> photos | Photos: let the phone search. | color key

Photos. Don't spend evenings sorting thousands of pictures into albums. Most phones already search by place, date, and even what's in the picture. Sort only the few you'll show other people.

## The takeaway

> SCENE (photo): A messy desk.
> desk2 | Order is an investment, not a virtue. | color key

[calm] Order isn't a virtue. It's an investment. Spend it where you'll search often. Everywhere else, a pile is fine.

[warmly] So the next time someone teases you about your messy desk, you can tell them it's not a mess. It's an algorithm.
