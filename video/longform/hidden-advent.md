## The wheel

> SCENE (photo): Hamburg; a snowy street; children at a window.
> hamburg | Hamburg, 1839 | color key
> snow-street |  | color @ a young theologian
> kids-a |  | color @ Every December

[calm] Hamburg, 1839. Johann Hinrich Wichern, a young theologian, ran a home for poor children called the [[Rauhes Haus]]. Every December, the children asked him the same thing. How many days until Christmas?

> SCENE (photo): The wheel; candles.
> wheel-a | Four white candles for the Sundays. A red one for every other day. | color key
> red-candle |  | color @ four big white candles

[warmly] So Wichern found an old wagon wheel and hung it from the ceiling. On it he put four big white candles, one for each Sunday of Advent. And a small red candle for every day in between, all the way to the twenty-fourth.

> SCENE (photo): Lighting candles; the wheel glowing.
> candle-match |  | color
> wheel-b |  | color @ brighter every day

[warmly] Each evening, the children gathered, sang, and lit one more candle. The room got brighter every day. They could see Christmas coming.

[calm] A countdown you could light.

## From wheel to wreath

> SCENE (photo): Fir branches; a wreath on a table.
> snow-forest |  | color
> wreath2 | Fir branches: around 1860 | color key @ around 1860
> wreath3 |  | color @ only the four big ones

[calm] The fir branches came later, around 1860. And the wheel shrank. Two dozen candles don't fit on a kitchen table, so families kept only the four big ones, one for each Sunday.

> SCENE (photo): A church in the snow; a wreath with four candles.
> snow-church |  | color
> candle-snow |  | color @ Catholic churches
> wreath4 |  | color @ nearly every home

[calm] The wreath spread through Protestant northern Germany first. Catholic churches took it up in the twentieth century. Today you'll find one in nearly every home, office and shop.

> SCENE (list): Four Sundays, four candles.
> bg | candle
> First Advent | one candle
> Second Advent | two @ On the second
> Fourth Advent | all four @ By the fourth

On the first Sunday of Advent, you light one candle. On the second, two. By the fourth, all four are burning, and Christmas is days away.

## The biscuits

> SCENE (photo): A snowy window; the biscuits on the cardboard.
> snow-window | Swabia, the 1880s | color key
> biscuits-a | Twenty-four biscuits, one a day. | color key @ his mother
> cookies |  | color @ One a day

[warmly] Now the calendar. In the eighteen-eighties, a boy called Gerhard Lang grew up in Swabia. Before Christmas, his mother put twenty-four little biscuits on a piece of cardboard. One a day. That was it.

> SCENE (photo): A town; a desk by a window; a calendar.
> oldtown-roofs | Munich | color key
> desk-window |  | color @ printed the first Advent calendars
> calendar | Printed calendars: 1903 to 1908 | color key @ exact year

[calm] Gerhard grew up, became a printer in Munich, and remembered the biscuits. In the early nineteen-hundreds, he printed the first Advent calendars: sheets of pictures you cut out and glued on, one for each day. Sources argue about the exact year, somewhere between 1903 and 1908.

> SCENE (photo): Chocolate.
> chocolate | Chocolate behind the doors: 1958 | color key @ Not until

[calm] Little doors came later. And chocolate behind every door? Not until 1958.

> SCENE (photo): Biscuits by candlelight.
> cookies2 |  | color
> anise | One mother. One piece of cardboard. | color key @ one mother

[warmly] So every chocolate calendar in every supermarket goes back to one mother, one piece of cardboard, and a boy who couldn't wait.

## The market

> SCENE (photo): The medieval market; Dresden.
> rooftops |  | color
> market-a | Dresden, 1434 | color key @ In 1434
> market-b |  | color @ Monday before Christmas
> snow-window2 |  | color @ meat market

[calm] The Christmas market is older than both. In 1434, a document from Dresden mentions a market on the Monday before Christmas. It was mostly a meat market, so people could buy their Christmas roast.

> SCENE (photo): The Striezelmarkt today.
> striezel2 | Striezelmarkt | color key
> dresden-night |  | color @ Stollen
> blue-wonder |  | color @ oldest documented

[calm] It became the [[Striezelmarkt]], named after [[Striezel]], an old word for the bread we now call [[Stollen]]. That's why Dresden calls itself the oldest documented Christmas market in Germany.

> SCENE (list): Who was first?
> bg | snow-town
> Dresden | 1434, a Christmas market
> Bautzen | 1384, a royal charter @ royal charter
> Historians | not sure @ Historians

[warmly] [[Bautzen]], about fifty kilometres east, disagrees. It has a royal charter from 1384, fifty years earlier, allowing a meat market there too. Was that already a Christmas market, or just a market in winter? Historians aren't sure. The two towns are still arguing.

## A letter about butter

> SCENE (photo): Stollen; a medieval bakery.
> sugar | Stollen | color key
> oil-a |  | color @ fasting
> oil-b |  | color @ with oil

[warmly] And what do you eat at a market in Saxony? Stollen. But for a long time, Stollen was not very nice. [calm] Advent used to be a time of fasting, and fasting meant no butter. So bakers made their Stollen with oil. And the oil back then was bitter.

> SCENE (photo): Dough; the seal; the messenger.
> dough |  | color
> letter-b | The Butterbrief, 1491 | color key @ Pope Innocent
> butter-pan |  | color @ the butter letter
> oven-fire | Pay a fee. Build a cathedral. | color key @ there was a catch

[calm] The rulers of Saxony asked the Pope for permission to use butter. It took years. In 1491, Pope Innocent the Eighth finally answered, in a letter now called the [[Butterbrief]], the butter letter. Yes, you may use butter. But there was a catch. Pay a fee, and the money goes towards the cathedral in [[Freiberg]].

> SCENE (photo): Flour and dough.
> flour |  | color

[warmly] So the butter in your Stollen helped to pay for a church.

## The giant Stollen

> SCENE (photo): Dresden; the baroque camp; the giant Stollen.
> dresden-steamer | August the Strong | color key
> giant-a | Zeithain, 1730 | color key @ In 1730
> giant-b | About seven metres long | color key @ seven metres

[calm] Then came August the Strong, ruler of Saxony, a man who did nothing small. In 1730 he put on an enormous military show near the village of [[Zeithain]], with tens of thousands of guests. To end it, his bakers made a Stollen about seven metres long.

> SCENE (photo): The Stollen festival in Dresden today.
> stollen-fest | Dresden's Stollenfest | color key
> dresden-skate |  | color @ every Advent

[warmly] Dresden hasn't forgotten. Every Advent, its Stollen festival carries a giant Stollen through the old town, and cuts it up for the crowd.

## The oldest Glühwein cup

> SCENE (photo): A castle on the Rhine; the tankard.
> rhine-castle | The Rhine, about 1420 | color key
> tankard-a |  | color @ a count on the Rhine
> tankard-b | A silver cup for hot spiced wine | color key @ gold-plated

[calm] And to drink? [[Glühwein]], hot spiced red wine. People heated wine with spices back in Roman times. But the oldest known Glühwein cup in Germany belonged to a count on the Rhine, around 1420. A gold-plated silver tankard, made for hot spiced wine.

> SCENE (photo): Steam from a cup; cinnamon.
> steam-cup |  | color
> cinnamon-cup |  | color @ Riesling

[warmly] The same count, the story goes, was among the first to plant Riesling grapes. A man who knew his wine, hot or cold.
